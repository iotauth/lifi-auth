Each block below is: which slide it's for, what the image shows in one

1. system_block_diagram.png  —  Slide 5, "System Architecture"
------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT IT SHOWS: The three-participant security model (Sender, Receiver,
Verifier) on top, with the optical link called out in orange; the real
deployed reporting path (Pi4 -> WiFi -> dashboard) shown de-emphasized
in gray underneath as a separate, non-security-relevant layer.

SAY (~60-75s):
"This is the system architecture. There are three participants. On the
left, the optical beacon — a Pico RP2040 that encrypts every message
with AES-128-GCM and drives four LED channels in parallel over a custom
PIO transmitter. On the right, the client device — a Pico 2, RP2350 —
with a photodiode front-end that demodulates the signal and checks each
frame against a 64-entry nonce window before it's ever decrypted. And
at the top, the key provisioning service — the IoTAuth server — which
both sides authenticate to independently over TLS with X.509 certs.

The orange arrow is the one that matters for this thesis: that's the
optical channel, and it's one-way, and it's the thing the whole security
argument is built on — physical light, not a software boundary.

Below the divider is the actual deployed hardware path — the receiver
reports over WiFi with HMAC-signed requests to a dashboard, so there's
a human-visible verified/revoked state. I've drawn that separately and
grayed out on purpose, because it's implementation, not part of the
security model above it — and I'll come back to why that distinction
matters when I get to the evaluation methodology."

WHY IT'S DRAWN THIS WAY (if asked): the top row maps 1:1 to the paper's
System Model section so the threat model slide right after it doesn't
need re-explaining; the bottom row exists because a sharp committee
member will ask "how does this actually get validated/observed" and you
want the answer on screen before they ask it.

2. revocation_latency.png  —  Slide 12, "G4 — Time-to-revoke on occlusion"
------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT IT SHOWS: Two strip plots (one per trial batch) of time-to-revoke
(left) and time-to-reverify (right) on physical occlusion, dashed line
at the production Δ=15s / heartbeat 2.5s reference.

SAY (~75-90s):
"This is the occlusion test — physically blocking the optical path and
timing how long it takes the receiver to revoke authorization, then
unblocking and timing re-verification. I ran two batches: the top row,
in blue, is the as-deployed condition — dashboard polling every 5
seconds, which is production behavior. The bottom row, in orange, polls
every 0.2 seconds instead, to separate the mechanism's real latency from
polling artifacts.

Look at the blue row first: it's bimodal — two clusters around 15.7 and
18.2 seconds. That's not the protocol misbehaving, that's aliasing
against the 5-second poll interval. You can see that the moment you look
at the orange row: once polling is fine-grained, everything collapses
into a tight cluster at 15.11 seconds, standard deviation six
hundredths of a second — essentially exactly Δ, the 15-second freshness
window, plus about a tenth of a second of fixed processing overhead.
That's the evidence that G4 — revocation on interruption — is enforced
precisely, and that the extra spread you'd see in a naive as-deployed
measurement is a monitoring artifact, not a security property.

On the right, re-verification after unblocking is consistently under
one and a half seconds in both batches, bounded above by the 2.5-second
heartbeat interval, which is exactly what you'd expect."

IF ASKED "why two batches, isn't that redundant": say directly that the
as-deployed numbers are what a user actually experiences, but they're not
what the mechanism actually guarantees — the fine-grained batch isolates
that guarantee from the measurement tool.

3. delta_sensitivity.png  —  Slide 13, "Δ-sensitivity to benign traffic"
------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT IT SHOWS: Log-scale histogram of 959 inter-frame gaps from a 40-min
benign session, all clustered near 2.5s, with six candidate Δ values
(3-30s) marked and each showing 0.0% false-revoke rate.

SAY (~60-75s, deliver this one straight, don't oversell it):
"This experiment asks a different question: is Δ=15 seconds actually
justified, or just a number someone picked? I logged 959 gaps between
valid frames over a 40-minute benign session and checked, for six
candidate Δ values from 3 to 30 seconds, what fraction of those gaps
would have caused a false revocation.

The honest result is on screen: every single gap clustered within about
20 milliseconds of the 2.5-second heartbeat interval — that spike on
the left — so every candidate Δ, including the most aggressive one at
3 seconds, comes back with a zero percent false-revoke rate. I want to
be direct about why that's not as strong a result as it looks: this
session only has one traffic pattern in it, a mechanically regular
heartbeat. It doesn't contain the kind of irregular gaps you'd get from
partial occlusion or marginal alignment in a real degraded environment,
so it can't be used to find where a real crossover would be. What it
does establish is a floor: Δ=15 seconds costs nothing against the
traffic pattern I could measure. A full parametric justification under
degraded conditions is future work, and I say that in the paper rather
than let the clean zero-percent number imply more than it shows."

IF ASKED "so why include a null result": because reporting it honestly —
including what it doesn't prove — is stronger evidence of rigor than
omitting a result that didn't come out as informative as hoped.

4. replay_boundary.png  —  Slide 14, "G2 — Replay rejection + boundary limitation"
------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT IT SHOWS: A single-trial event timeline — frame captured/pinned,
immediate replay rejected (green +), nonce window deliberately shrunk to
2 slots, two legitimate frames cycle through evicting the pinned nonce,
then the same replayed frame is accepted again (amber X).

SAY (~75-90s):
"This is the replay test, and it's really two results in one timeline.
At t plus zero, a legitimate heartbeat frame arrives and I pin a copy of
its raw bytes. At t plus 0.21 seconds, I immediately re-inject that
exact same frame — and it's rejected, the green marker — because its
nonce is already in the receiver's replay window. That's G2 working
exactly as designed.

Then I deliberately shrink the nonce window from its production size of
64 down to just 2 slots, to make a boundary condition observable in a
short test instead of needing thousands of frames. Two more legitimate
frames arrive — messages 52 and 53 — and because the window only holds
2 entries now, the original frame's nonce gets evicted. At t plus 5
seconds, I replay that exact same original frame a third time, and this
time — the amber X — it's accepted and successfully decrypted.

That's not a bug I found by accident, it's a real, documented boundary
of the sliding-window design: replay protection is bounded by window
capacity, not permanent. In production, at 64 slots, an attacker would
need 64 fresh legitimate frames to cycle through before a captured
frame becomes replayable again — which at the sender's minimum
authenticated rate bounds how long a captured frame stays a live
threat — but the limitation is real, and I'm putting it on the same
slide as the positive result instead of only in a footnote."

IF ASKED "isn't a 2-slot window unrealistic": say plainly that it's an
artificially small window chosen only to make the boundary condition
observable in a short live demo — the mechanism, and the limitation, are
the same at any window size, just slower to trigger at 64.

5. baud_sweep.png  —  Slide 15, "Communication performance"
------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT IT SHOWS: Frame success rate vs. baud rate for two TIA feedback
resistor configurations (470kΩ, 100kΩ), each with a clean ceiling, a
measured degradation region, and a hard collapse point.

SAY (~60-75s):
"This is the baud-rate sweep behind the bandwidth-range tradeoff in the
paper. Two configurations of the transimpedance amplifier's feedback
resistor: blue is 470 kiloohms — higher gain, lower bandwidth, 2-volt
output swing. Orange is 100 kiloohms — lower gain, higher bandwidth,
400-millivolt swing.

Both hold 100% frame success up to their ceiling — 300 kilobaud for
470k, 250 kilobaud for 100k — then the orange line shows measurable
degradation, 96% and 86%, before both configurations hit a hard
collapse, marked with the X, where control messages themselves get
corrupted and the link stops working entirely.

The result that matters here is a little counterintuitive: at this
close range, 15 centimeters, the higher-gain 470k configuration
actually outperforms the higher-bandwidth 100k configuration. That
tells us SNR margin from the larger voltage swing is the limiting
factor at short range, not the TIA's raw bandwidth — the classic
bandwidth-range tradeoff only starts to favor the higher-bandwidth
config once you're far enough away that photocurrent drops and the
400-millivolt swing stops clearing the comparator's noise floor. That's
also why it's future work rather than a claim in this thesis — I
haven't measured that crossover distance yet."
